Our story

We built the retreat so the elephants would never have to earn their keep.

Lovie’s Haven began with a simple, uncomfortable observation: almost every place that rescues elephants ends up needing them to perform in some way — a bath, a walk, a photograph — in order to survive.

The problem we couldn't get past

An elephant costs somewhere between seventy and a hundred thousand dollars a year to keep well. Food, veterinary care, foot care, land, water, fencing, staff, insurance, and the long tail of geriatric medicine for an animal that may live another three decades.

Most sanctuaries fund that by selling access. It starts gently — a viewing platform, a feeding station — and it ends with an animal being moved, held, cleaned or coaxed on a schedule that belongs to a ticket, not to her. Nobody sets out to do it. The economics do it for them.

We wanted a place where the money could never reach the elephants' day.

The idea

Fund the sanctuary with something people already want.

People will pay for rest. They will pay for a quiet cabin, a glass room at sunrise, an unhurried week, a wedding somewhere that means something. None of that requires an elephant to do anything at all.

So we inverted the model. The retreat is the business. The sanctuary is the purpose. Guests come for their own restoration and leave having funded someone else's — and the herd, a kilometre away behind the treeline, never knows they were here.

What we promise

That the elephants who arrive here have made their last journey. That nobody will ask them to be useful again. That they will be free to be dull, unsociable, invisible and old, and still be completely welcome.

And that the people who come here to breathe will leave carrying a little of that responsibility with them.

Rehabilitation for elephants.
Restoration for people.

The name

Named for the first one through the gate.

Lovie spent thirty-one years in a travelling circus. In the autumn of 2023 a truck door opened and she walked — slowly, on damaged feet — onto land that would never ask her for anything again.

She stood at the treeline for four days before she went in. We named the place while watching her do it.

The part we didn't expect

The people turned out to need it too.

We built the retreat as a funding mechanism. Then guests started staying an extra night, and writing to us afterwards, and coming back on the anniversary of something.

An elephant recovering from thirty years in a circus and a person recovering from a year they would rather not discuss are not the same thing, and we will never say they are. But they respond to the same conditions: room to move, nothing demanded, and time measured in seasons instead of sessions.

That is the whole design. Two species, one set of needs, one hundred and fifty acres.

Come once as a guest. Stay for life as a protector.
