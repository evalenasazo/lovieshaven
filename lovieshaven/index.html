The one rule

Is this better for the elephants?

Every decision here answers that question first — every building, every path, every booking, every dollar. If the answer is no, we don't build it. That single rule is why there are no rides, no shows, no bathing and no touching — and why, when the herd grazes the meadow in front of the pavilion most evenings, it is because they chose the grass, not because anyone arranged it.

How the rule works in practice

The land

One estate, laid out around the herd.

A hundred and fifty acres in central Florida: a conservation zone and a forest grove for the elephants, a lake with its own watering hole, the open Elephant View Meadow, and the retreat threaded carefully along the water's edge.

Guests live on the lake side — the Sky View Pavilion at the centre, the lakeside cabins and the guest village around it, the Sunset Dock for the end of the day. The herd's ground runs north and east: the grove, the conservation zone, the watering hole, and the open meadow they cross between them.

The layout does the work. Elephants come to the meadow and the water because elephants like meadows and water. The pavilion simply happens to face them when they do.

Two species, one mission

Everyone here is recovering from something.

Our elephants arrive from circuses, roadside display, riding camps and closed zoos. Our guests arrive from burnout, grief, anxiety and the kind of year that doesn’t end cleanly.

We are not going to pretend those are the same wound. But the treatment turns out to look alike: space, quiet, time, good food, no demands, and nobody watching you to see whether you’re better yet.

They rest from

Circuses, riding camps, roadside attractions, neglect, decades of being useful

You rest from

Burnout, stress, anxiety, grief, a transition you didn’t choose

Shared prescription

Land, water, shade, silence, an unhurried day, and no obligation to perform

The Sky View Pavilion

A glass room at the edge of the meadow.

The Sky View Pavilion is the heart of the estate — wellness, events and dining under one timber-and-glass roof, set where the gardens end and the Elephant View Meadow begins. Yoga at sunrise. Long-table lunches. Sound baths after dark. Talks by the people who care for the herd.

Most evenings the herd grazes somewhere in the view. Some evenings they come all the way down to the watering hole. The hour is unhurried either way.

Winnie’s Farm

The rescue barn takes all comers.

The elephants are not the only residents. Winnie’s Farm, on the east side of the estate, takes in rescued goats, sheep, donkeys and elderly horses — animals with nowhere else to go and, unlike the herd, a real enthusiasm for company.

It is the one place at Lovie’s Haven where hands-on is the whole idea. The elephants keep their distance because they have earned it. The goats never got the memo.

How this is paid for

We sell everything except the elephants.

Sanctuaries that depend on visitors seeing animals eventually start managing animals so visitors see them. We removed that pressure by building the revenue somewhere else.

Cabins, memberships, retreats, weddings, the café, the shop, the farm, donations, grants and sponsorships all carry the cost of care. No income line anywhere in this business depends on an elephant performing, posing, or coming a step closer than she chooses to.

Where the money goes

  • Cabin stays and private buyouts
  • Wellness memberships, local and remote
  • Hosted and private retreats
  • Weddings and small ceremonies
  • The café and the gift shop
  • Winnie’s Farm programmes and produce
  • Individual and monthly donations
  • Named sponsorships and grants
  • The care endowment, held for the long term
What we're actually building

An elephant can live sixty years. We are planning for the last thirty of them.

Lovie’s Haven is not trying to be the biggest sanctuary. It is trying to be the one that is still standing, still funded, and still worthy of trust when the animals we take in today are old.
