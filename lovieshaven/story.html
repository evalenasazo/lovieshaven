Our story

I've loved elephants my whole life. This place is named for the person I loved most.

The elephants came first — a lifelong obsession, the kind where every trip I've ever truly wanted to take ends at a sanctuary somewhere in Asia. But Lovie’s Haven really began with something much harder: losing my best friend.

Lovie

Lovie was my cousin, and my best friend.

She passed away far too soon, and I did what people do with a grief that size: I built something that could hold it. The Lovie’s Foundation was created after her death, in her honor, for the benefit of her daughter — so the person she loved most will always be taken care of.

Lovie’s Haven is an extension of that foundation. Same name, same reason, bigger dream: a place where the animals I've loved my whole life are cared for the same way — completely, permanently, and with a love that never asks for anything back.

The Lovie’s Foundation

Storybooks that grow up with a child.

The foundation writes children's books — animal adventures, the kind I would have read aloud to Lovie’s daughter myself. Every dollar they earn belongs to her future.

The proceeds go into a high-yield savings account and stay there, growing, until she turns twenty-one. Money like that can change a life or dent one, so the foundation prepares its children for it years in advance — and only hands over the funds when they're ready to hold them. It began with one child, Lovie’s daughter, and it works the same way for every child the foundation takes on.

The problem we couldn't stop thinking about

An elephant costs somewhere between seventy and a hundred thousand dollars a year to keep well. Food, veterinary care, foot care, land, water, fencing, staff, insurance, and the long tail of geriatric medicine for an animal that may live another three decades.

Most sanctuaries cover those costs by selling access. It starts gently — a viewing platform, a feeding station — and little by little, an animal ends up being moved, held, cleaned or coaxed on a schedule that belongs to a ticket instead of to her. Nobody sets out to do that to an animal they love. The bills do it for them.

We wanted to build a place where the bills could never, ever reach the elephants' day.

The idea

Fund the sanctuary with something people already want.

People will happily pay for rest — a quiet cabin, a sunrise yoga class in a glass pavilion, an unhurried week, a wedding somewhere that truly means something. And here's the beautiful part: none of that asks an elephant to do anything at all.

So we flipped the model. The retreat is the business; the sanctuary is the reason for everything. Guests come here to rest and heal, and every night they stay helps an elephant do the same. Most evenings the herd grazes past the pavilion glass — but only ever because they feel like it.

What we promise

Every elephant who arrives here has made her last journey. Nobody will ever ask her to work, perform, or pose again. She can spend her days exactly how she pleases — social or solitary, out in the meadow or hidden in the trees — and she will be deeply loved either way.

And we promise the people who come here to rest will leave carrying a little of that love with them.

Rehabilitation for elephants.
Restoration for people.

The name

There are two Lovies here now.

Our first elephant spent thirty-one years in a traveling circus. In the fall of 2023, a truck door opened and she stepped out — slowly, on tired feet — onto land that would never ask her for anything again. She had a name waiting for her before she ever arrived.

She stood at the treeline for four days before she trusted it enough to walk in. We watched her do it, and when she finally disappeared into the trees she was carrying my cousin's name with her — and making it a home.

It's on everything now: the haven, the foundation, and Lovie’s residence on the north lake, where our family lives year-round on the land we protect.

The part we didn't expect

The people turned out to need it too.

Honestly, we built the retreat to pay for elephant care. Then guests started staying an extra night. They wrote to us weeks later. They came back on the anniversary of something. Somewhere along the way we realized we'd built a healing place for people, too.

An elephant recovering from thirty years in a circus and a person recovering from a hard year are not the same, and we'd never say they were. But they heal in the same conditions: room to move, nothing demanded of them, and time measured in sunrises instead of sessions.

That's the whole design. Two species, one set of needs, 150 beautiful acres.

Come once as a guest. Stay for life as a protector.
