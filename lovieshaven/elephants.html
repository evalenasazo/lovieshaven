Our elephants

They've worked their whole lives. Now their only job is being elephants.

Every elephant at Lovie’s Haven came from a life of work — circuses, rides, logging, roadside attractions. Here they have land, water, mud, shade, friends if they want them, and all the peace and quiet they could ever ask for. This is their retirement, and they've earned every acre of it.

How you'll come to know them

Through their stories — and, most days, right across the meadow.

You'll come to know these elephants better in a week here than you ever could at a fence — partly because we love telling their stories, and partly because the meadow they graze runs right up to the pavilion glass. Most days, they're simply there, living their lives in full view.

Every evening in the pavilion, one of our keepers tells the story of a single resident: where she came from, what she went through, what she was like when she arrived, and who she's become since. There's footage from the habitat cameras, a conservation talk every afternoon, and — whenever it happens naturally — the chance to watch everyday care, like morning feeding or a health check, from the barn's viewing wall.

The land itself makes the introductions. The watering hole and the Elephant View Meadow sit in front of the Sky View Pavilion because the herd loves that ground, and we built the glass to face it. We never bring an elephant to you — we simply gave you a front-row seat to where they already love to be.

  • You will never touch an elephant here
  • You will never feed, bathe or walk with one
  • No elephant will ever be moved so that you can see her
  • No elephant will ever be summoned, positioned or lit for a better view
  • If she leaves the meadow, we let her go

We know some of these are unusual promises for a sanctuary to make. They're exactly why the elephants here get to heal. Wondering how sightings actually work? We answer that plainly.

The residents

Meet the herd: four elephants with about 140 working years behind them.

We'll keep three to five residents at a time — that's what 150 acres holds honestly, and we'd rather give a small herd a big life than the other way around. A new elephant is welcomed only when we know we can care for her for the rest of her life, which can mean thirty years or more.

Lovies

Asian  ·  47  ·  Arrived 2023  ·  From a travelling circus

Lovies spent thirty-one years on the road with a circus. She came to us underweight, with chronic foot damage and a nervous sway that, little by little, she's stopped needing. She was the first elephant to set foot on this land, and she carries the most important name we have — Lovies, for the founder's cousin and best friend, whose story is the reason this place exists. She spent her first four days standing at the treeline, just looking, before she trusted it enough to walk in.

These days she takes the long way around the lake every morning, for no reason except that she can. Watching her do it never gets old.

Bala

Asian  ·  52  ·  Arrived 2024  ·  From a tourist riding camp

Bala carried tourists on her back for twenty-six years, and her spine will always show it. She's the most cautious of our four girls and the slowest to trust someone new — and honestly, who could blame her? We let her set the pace.

She bonded with Lovies within two weeks of arriving, and in the two years since, the pair have hardly ever been more than a hundred yards apart. Best friends, plain and simple.

Meera

Asian  ·  39  ·  Arrived 2025  ·  From a closed roadside attraction

Meera spent nineteen years alone in an enclosure smaller than our pavilion. When she arrived, she didn't know what mud was for. It took eleven months before she finally tried the wallow — and the keeper who watched it happen sat down in the grass and cried.

She's still deciding whether she likes other elephants. That's okay. She has all the time in the world.

Ruth

African  ·  58  ·  Arrived 2025  ·  From a zoo closure

Ruth is the eldest of the herd and the reason we built the retirement barn. She has arthritis in both front legs, and most mornings she walks herself in for warmth, foot care and pain relief — always by her own choice. She has never once been made to come in, and she never will be.

Guests who watch her care sessions from the viewing wall tend to go quiet for a while afterward. She has that effect on people.

The field log

Real life at the sanctuary, published every day.

Our care team writes down what actually happens here each day, and we publish it word for word — the magical evenings when the herd grazes right up to the pavilion lights, and the quiet mornings when they'd rather keep to the forest. We share both, because both are the truth.

14 July06:40

The herd was already at the watering hole when the sunrise class arrived. Lovies stood in the shallows for most of an hour. Twenty-two people practiced very slowly. Nobody called them in. They just like mornings there.

12 July17:20

Meera went into the far wallow for the first time in eleven days and stayed forty minutes, in full view of the meadow trail. Nobody called anybody over. Nobody needed to be closer.

11 July13:05

Browse delivery — willow and bamboo, dropped at four points across the range so nobody has to share if they don't want to. Bala got there first, as usual, and then didn't eat any of it.

09 July05:55

Foot care for Ruth, ninety minutes, voluntary. She chose to come in and she chose to leave. Six guests watched the session from the barn viewing wall and asked good questions afterwards.

07 July19:10

Rain all day. The herd spent it under the old oaks at the boundary, completely content. We canceled nothing, because there was nothing scheduled to cancel.

04 July21:30

Wedding in the meadow, a hundred and twenty guests, ended at nine as agreed. Sound meter at the habitat line read below ambient all evening. Lovies and Meera were at the south lake and did not move once.
